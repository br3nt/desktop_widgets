# Codex — skin brainstorm, stage 1

These are alternatives to react to, not a settled visual system. The strongest opportunity is to make docking and shading reveal how each object was made: masonry locks, wool compresses, silver flexes, wire winds, and drawn shells nest. A material should change the choreography as much as the palette.

## Ground rules for the exploration

Read: `docs/skins/BRIEF.md`, `README.md`, `docs/skin-formats.md`, `prototypes/capsule-study.html`, and the linked local Connectables idea note. The six supplied photographs correspond to `refs/3.png` through `refs/8.png`.

Keep Jarvis, GitDiscuss and Checks, with the prototype's content and independent state. Jarvis still has Status / Task / Log, “03 agents running,” its activity drawer and concurrency control. GitDiscuss still has Queue / Thread / Log and “02 need a decision.” Checks still reports “12 / 14” and “2 waiting for approval.” No fantasy replacement copy. The drawer stays with Jarvis; Checks remains independently removable and gains no end sockets merely because its casing changes.

Distinguish **fusion**, where inner ornament withdraws into one housing, from **attachment**, where two recognisable objects meet at a designed joint. Containment remains a third behaviour. All joins below concern physical assembly, with no implied workflow or permission changes.

Keep the accepted approach feedback: 120 px near, 40 px ready, measured from the raw pose to a compatible target pose. Each aesthetic may express that feedback through a different small detail, but proximity cannot perform the latch before release. When moving away, the same feedback reverses without a hidden lockout. Longer material animations begin after commitment; they do not make the drag sluggish. A joined group moves by either nameplate; each joint has an identifiable release control and a keyboard equivalent.

### Shared shade and shape rules

- Explore a roughly 30–36 px visual strip first, with separately sized control targets. Each strip keeps the widget name, its primary status and an expand control. Jarvis can say “03 agents running,” GitDiscuss “02 need a decision,” Checks “12 / 14 checks passed.” Preserve the underlying pages, pause state and pending counts. A focused readout can expose full text without continuous scrolling.
- Hide the expanded display only after its summary is available. Never compress live text into squashed glyphs. Expanding restores the previous page and focus sensibly; controls hidden by shade become inert.
- Close Jarvis's drawer visually before shading and remember whether it was open. Loaded Checks stays loaded, with a labelled drawer indicator on Jarvis's strip; independent Checks shades independently. Restore the remembered drawer state when space permits.
- Anchor a form change at the active joint or the dragged edge. Resize the casing around stable displays, then reflow content at authored size thresholds. A different silhouette does not require an arbitrary continuous path morph.
- Angles are planar and user-set. Show allowable arcs only while adjusting. Each socket declares its range; zero-angle fusion may change to an articulated attachment at other angles. Preview collisions with neighbours and the drawer. Never imply that a carved stone seam can bend.
- If one docked widget shades alone, retain a small socket carrier at the existing joint; the thin body can sit above or below it. Group shade can align all strips together. This is less surprising than moving an entire chain to follow one shrinking centre.
- Future studies should freeze **separate**, **joining**, **joined**, and **shade**, with independent selectors for join and shade alternatives. A halfway pose must communicate the mechanism without requiring motion. Reduced motion jumps directly to the equivalent state.

## 1. Gothic cathedral / basilica

### What makes it itself

Weight and repetition, light entering through constrained openings, deep carved reveals, load-bearing ribs, and small human-scale fittings inside monumental forms. Picture two long limestone reliquaries with dark recessed displays, restrained brass controls, and narrow stained-glass clerestories above them. One can lean pale and sunlit; another can use soot-dark stone and wine glass. Cathedral ornament need not mean spikes everywhere.

Try three distinct silhouettes: a low basilica arcade with repeated round arches; a severe pointed-arch instrument with a few deep cuts; a carved frieze slab with a single sculpted end figure. The figure belongs on an outer cap, never between connected screens. Motion has a short, heavy stop, while transmitted glass colour briefly slides across the nearby stone.

**Cheap version:** a black rectangle, a gothic font and repeated pointed arches pasted on top. **Overboard version:** full miniature towers, statues at every corner, text behind stained glass, flying buttresses covering adjacent widgets. Keep the architectural hierarchy: large quiet mass, medium structural rhythm, a few tiny incisions.

### Join alternatives

1. **Keystone lock — fusion.** Approach: two recessed half-arches brighten along their bevels. Commit: the inner capitals sink, the casings meet, and a short wedge descends into the arch crown. Joined seam: a narrow central pier with one brass notch to lift the key and release. The outer arcade reads as one long building. The satisfying moment is the final downward seating, not an elastic bounce.
2. **Flying buttress — external attachment.** Approach: two corbels reveal pale contact faces across a deliberate gap. Commit: a shallow stone rib swings in-plane from one shoulder and seats in the other. Joined seam: open desktop beneath the bridge, with each casing clearly intact. A small hinge cap is the release target. Limit the bridge to the upper shoulder so it cannot cover either display.
3. **Lead-came solder — surface fusion.** Approach: tiny glass panes at the two ends show matching lead profiles. Commit: panes slide aside and a lead line draws downward along the meeting edge; a single warm highlight travels with it and stops. Joined seam: a thin dark metal mullion bordered by two jewel-coloured slivers. Separation parts the mullion cleanly; no broken-glass effect. This version reads as window construction rather than masonry.
4. **Iron strap and pin — mechanical attachment.** Approach: forged hinge knuckles line up, their worn edges becoming visible. Commit: a vertical pin drops through the alternating knuckles and a flat strap settles against each stone cheek. Joined seam: black iron against pale stone, with a small ring at the pin head. Pulling the ring withdraws the pin. Good for the utilitarian monastery variant.
5. **Radial apse joint — rotating attachment.** Approach: a circular medallion on one end approaches a semicircular stone seat. Commit: two crescent jaws close around the medallion without spinning either display. Joined seam: a shallow rose-shaped hub, with radial ticks visible during angle adjustment. Unlike the strap, this is explicitly an adjustable pivot.
6. **Frieze dovetail — sliding fusion.** Approach: alternating carved courses reveal a stepped fit. Commit: one end collar translates a few pixels along its channel; courses interleave and outer mouldings become continuous. Joined seam: a quiet zigzag mortar line, with the release concealed in one visibly bevelled block. More horizontal and basilica-like than the keystone; avoid turning it into a puzzle the user must solve.

### Shade alternatives

1. **Clerestory strip.** The lower walls rise behind the top cornice; the main display gives way to a single line beneath a row of narrow glass lights. Restore by lowering the walls from the same cornice. Best everyday option: the strip remains architectural at very small height.
2. **Folding triptych.** Two carved side leaves fold over the expanded content, then the closed panel lowers into a shallow horizontal nameplate. Restore raises the panel and opens both leaves. Use a brief illustrated fold, not perspective-distorted text. Final strip: brass-edged dark wood with one central glass lozenge.
3. **Stone course stack.** Three horizontal casing courses move upward and overlap like telescoping masonry. Restore separates the courses, revealing the screen between them. Final strip: a thick lintel with two fine shadow grooves; the motion is sliding stone, not rubber compression.
4. **Slit shutter.** A dark iron shutter drops over the screen while the lower casing retracts behind it, leaving one illuminated inscription slot. Restore pulls the shutter up as the base extends. Final strip: austere stone ends and a charcoal band. Particularly good for the soot-dark variant.
5. **Frieze extraction.** The useful summary plate slides out along the top edge; the large ornamental casing withdraws behind that plate and disappears. Restore pulls the full casing back out from the plate. Final strip: a carved ribbon with the exact status inset into a flat field. More surprising than simply shrinking an arch.

### Morphs, angles and readable displays

A compact single arch can widen into a three-bay arcade; a tall form adds a second storey below the same cornice. Add bays at size thresholds rather than stretching statues or brick sizes. Checks is a small votive tablet that seats in the drawer's stone recess, with visible clearance and its own eject notch.

Keystone and dovetail fusion work at 0°. For user-set angles, use the medallion hub, initially exploring ±45°, or an iron hinge with a smaller range. A buttress can be a two-piece arch whose crown articulates; it must stop before its feet intersect either casing. These are proposed ranges to test, not inherited prototype capabilities.

Keep screens opaque ink, plum-black or warm ivory. Put glass colour in the reveal and casing, with no animated lighting across letters. Use ordinary system type for data; monumental lettering may label the casing only. A sunlit ivory display gives this aesthetic somewhere to go besides “dark fantasy.”

### Light construction and risks

Use reusable SVG arch and capital symbols, a few bevel gradients, and clipped flat colour for glass. Static seeded speckles can suggest stone; two offset strokes can suggest incisions. Start without filters; optionally test a small cached turbulence texture on the casing only. Translate rigid courses and keys instead of regenerating the whole shell during motion.

Risks: repeating arches become visual noise; carved corners consume display area; large shadows multiply across a group; tessellation can grow rapidly with ornate tracery. Keep high-detail motifs on the outer caps, share definitions, and replace tiny carving with plain grooves in shade. The baseline release target must remain easy to find amid ornament.

**First candidate:** pale keystone fusion + clerestory shade. **Stretch candidate:** glass-came fusion, which makes a joined pair feel like one illuminated window.

## 2. Felt

### What makes it itself

Dense needle-felted wool has soft volume, uneven compression, rounded cut edges and short fibres catching diffuse light. It is distinct from shiny rubber and from flat fabric with a stitched border. Picture an oatmeal felt pebble with a charcoal pressed-wool display patch, apricot button nubs and a moss-green pocket for Checks. A second direction is a dark indigo work pouch; a third is stacked pieces of coloured felt, with their thickness visible along the edges.

Motion should conserve the impression of stuffing: a compressed shoulder bulges slightly elsewhere, a pinched corner recovers once, a sewn flap bends at its attachment. The light is broad and quiet; a strong white specular highlight would undo the material.

**Cheap version:** pastel rounded cards plus noise and a dashed border. **Overboard version:** furry edges everywhere, continual squishing, wobbly text and mascot faces on every button. Give the wool convincing thickness and pressure points, then let it rest.

### Join alternatives

1. **Needle-felt merge — fusion.** Approach: two loose wool tufts lift toward one another. Commit: their edges compress together, fibres disappear into a narrow shared waist, and the silhouette settles into one long cushion. Joined seam: a shallow change in fibre direction, with a contrasting felt pull tab for release. Detachment parts the waist cleanly; no tearing sound or damaged wool.
2. **Button and elastic loop — point attachment.** Approach: a stitched loop stands up near a horn-coloured button. Commit: the loop stretches across, drops over the button and relaxes. Joined seam: a small open gap crossed by one loop. The button's broad backing makes the pivot plausible. Release lifts the loop using a generous invisible target.
3. **Zip closure — line attachment.** Approach: two fabric tapes align; short rows of soft-looking teeth become visible. Commit: a tiny pull travels down the seam, closing the teeth behind it. Joined seam: a narrow, slightly recessed stitched spine, with the pull parked at the lower edge. Useful for straight joins; not secretly a hinge.
4. **Hidden magnetic hems — face attachment.** Approach: two softly squared hems flatten toward each other. Commit: the hems meet with a small inward dimple, then return almost to their original volume. Joined seam: a straight dark crease with paired embroidered dots indicating the release area. This should feel quick and ordinary, not magical glowing wool.
5. **Blanket-stitch lacing — spanning attachment.** Approach: three reinforced edge holes on each side align. Commit: a thick thread draws through a short zigzag and the slack is taken up. Joined seam: three broad diagonal stitches bridging a sliver of desktop. A final loop is the release tab. Unjoining draws the thread back into its hem rather than leaving loose string.
6. **Sleeve cuff — enclosure attachment.** Approach: an open felt cuff on one widget shows its empty interior. Commit: the other widget's end enters and the cuff settles around it. Joined seam: an asymmetric double-thickness band, with the receiving widget visibly owning the cuff. This is neither a symmetric merge nor a point clasp; it suits a maker's pouch aesthetic.

### Shade alternatives

1. **Roll into a bolster.** The bottom edge curls upward, hiding the expanded content under a broad felt roll. Final strip: a soft horizontal bolster with a sewn-on summary tape. Restore unrolls downward. Only casing pieces curl; the display switches to its summary before it would be covered.
2. **Concertina pleats.** Three broad fabric folds gather beneath the top edge. Final strip: a flat label above a narrow scalloped lower edge. Restore pulls the lower hem down and opens the folds. More layered and tailored than the round bolster.
3. **Drawstring pouch.** Two side cords tighten and gather the lower body upward behind a rigid felt label. Final strip: a labelled mouth with small cord ends tucked at its corners. Restore loosens the gathers. The pouch must collapse vertically, not become a tiny unreadable ball.
4. **Pocket tuck.** The expanded widget slides into its own shallow top pocket; the pocket lip becomes the shade strip. Restore pulls it out by a large fabric tab. Final strip: a clean double-layer band, almost a textile index tab. This is the least animated everyday option.
5. **Press flat.** A soft dome compresses into a dense felt pad, with a slight sideways swell and one restrained recovery. Final strip: compressed layers around a flat dark label. Restore re-inflates the casing volume without scaling the text. Exaggeration should stop well before bouncing jelly.

### Morphs, angles and readable displays

A pebble widens by moving its seams apart; a tall form unfolds an extra fabric gusset. A large form can become a tool roll with distinct display pockets. Do not stretch the fibre pattern with the object: maintain fibre scale and add material. Checks remains a small padded insert in Jarvis's pocket-like drawer, with an exposed grip when open.

The loop-button joint can pivot around a broad sewn button; lacing can fan at a restrained angle. Try ±35° for those connections. A magnetic hem or zipper needs straight contact, so use an explicit short fabric hinge adapter when an angle is desired. The cuff can deform slightly, but cannot become an unlimited stretchy tether. Stops come from the contract, not a physics simulation.

Use dense, pressed-wool or smooth woven labels for displays. Keep stray fibres out of the content rectangle. Charcoal on oat, or cream on indigo, allows warmth without low contrast. Status can add an embroidered tick, but the actual words remain sharp system text. Flat accessible controls can sit on plush button bodies.

### Light construction and risks

Use a few rounded SVG shapes with broad gradients, one tiny seeded fibre tile and sparse edge strokes. Reuse the tile across the skin; change phase per object without generating thousands of fibres. Animate a handful of casing control points or layered silhouettes. Optional low-amplitude displacement belongs on an outer edge, never on the display.

Risks: blur and displacement can turn felt into foam, large filters cost more than the underlying shapes, and procedural fuzz can shimmer when moved. Prefer static texture coordinates and a crisp silhouette with a few irregularities. A fully rasterised per-widget felt texture would threaten memory goals; test a shared tile and filter-free fallback first.

**First candidate:** button-loop join + pocket-tuck shade. **Stretch candidate:** needle-felt fusion + bolster shade, where the material changes the entire object's behaviour.

## 3. Elven and intricate

### What makes it itself

Tension, taper and negative space. Fine chased silver, pale carved wood, translucent enamel, slender ribs and a few suspended stones. Ornament follows a structural line, splitting like a leaf vein and returning to it; it does not fill every available gap. Picture a long dark display held between two silver bows, with an asymmetrical crescent at one end and a single pale green stone at the other.

Explore a cool lunar instrument in silver and blue-black; a warm seed-vessel in bone-coloured wood and amber; and an almost calligraphic frame of dark metal around ivory. Elven need not always mean green leaves. Repeated ratios, finely split tips and graceful load paths can carry the identity without literal foliage or invented writing.

Light should skim an edge or sit inside enamel. Motion takes up tension, bends a little and settles into exact alignment. No perpetual sparkle cloud.

**Cheap version:** a generic fantasy panel with vines in its corners. **Overboard version:** hairline lace across the text, six layers of glow, unreadable runes and huge wings. Leave large areas empty so the few fine lines matter.

### Join alternatives

1. **Grafted ribs — fusion.** Approach: split silver tips part slightly to reveal matching channels. Commit: the tips slide into one another, then a small enamel collar closes around the overlap. Joined seam: a single branching rib with a pearl-sized collar at its centre. Release slides that collar back. More botanical graft than knot.
2. **Bowstring tension — suspended attachment.** Approach: two small eyelets reveal a faint straight alignment line. Commit: two fine cords extend, hook into opposite eyelets and draw taut, leaving a narrow open gap. Joined seam: a tiny triangular harp between the objects. A saddle bead releases the tension. The cords are decorative representations of a fixed joint, not simulated ropes that let the group wander.
3. **Iris clasp — enclosing attachment.** Approach: three long metal petals lift around an empty socket. Commit: the opposite polished end enters and the petals close around its collar. Joined seam: three overlapping tapered leaves around one circular throat. A distinct central rim turns to release; the widgets do not rotate during latching.
4. **Calligraphic ribbon — binding attachment.** Approach: two enamel tips point toward a common crossing. Commit: a flat silver ribbon makes one broad over-under loop around both collars. Joined seam: an open figure-eight silhouette, with the crossing kept well away from the displays. Release unwinds the single loop. This is flat strapwork with visible faces, unlike round wire coiling.
5. **Floating moonstone — field attachment.** Approach: paired crescents face a small empty space; their inner edges brighten. Commit: one pale stone glides from a recess into the gap and two fine arcs settle around it. Joined seam: an apparent air gap centred on the stone, with no solid bridge. The stone is also the release target. This is the deliberately mystical option; solid crescents and consistent state feedback must make the attachment understandable without constant glow.
6. **Inlaid scarf joint — flush fusion.** Approach: long tapered end faces show complementary pale and dark inlay. Commit: they slide together diagonally by a few pixels and become one uninterrupted outline. Joined seam: a slender diagonal silver vein across the frame, with a tiny raised release wedge. The beauty is near-invisibility, counterbalanced by a discoverable focus and hover treatment.

### Shade alternatives

1. **Pressed leaf.** The upper and lower bows approach until their tips meet, leaving a long almond-shaped strip with a flat central text field. Restore opens the bows. The frame changes curvature while glyphs retain their size.
2. **Quill sheath.** The large casing slides lengthwise into a slim silver spine; successive ribs disappear under one end cap. Final strip: a long quill-like instrument with two restrained end flourishes. Restore draws the ribs out. Keep sufficient width for the summary rather than shortening it into a pen nib.
3. **Fan of ribs.** Several framing ribs rotate in-plane around an end pivot and nest along a horizontal spine. Final strip: the spine and a small layered fan at one end. Restore fans the ribs out around the newly revealed display. The text never rides on the moving ribs.
4. **Enamel ribbon.** Upper and lower framing bands straighten and slide into a flat ribbon, as a translucent inner plate withdraws behind it. Final strip: pale enamel edged with two fine metallic lines. Restore bows the frame outward and returns the plate. This option emphasises crafted enamel rather than plants.
5. **Mist withdrawal.** The outer tracery loses opacity from its tips inward while the useful summary resolves on the retained central rail; the expanded body retracts into that rail. Restore grows the casing geometry first, then reveals its tracery. Final strip: dark rail, small stone, exact text. Avoid animated particles: the mystery comes from absence.

### Morphs, angles and readable displays

A long lens can open into a shield-like tall form or an oval reading frame. Keep the terminal stones unchanged and replace the rib arrangement between them at authored thresholds. Wide forms add one long uninterrupted display span; tall forms open a second content region rather than scaling everything down. Checks becomes a small enamel tablet in a carved drawer cradle.

The iris clasp offers a planar pivot, exploring ±50° behind its decorative petals. Bowstring geometry can recompute into a new small triangle for each chosen angle; it must stop before the cords cross a display. The scarf joint stays straight. The moonstone can use a bounded arc around the same socket centre, with static tick marks on focus so “magical” does not mean unbounded.

Use an opaque blue-black or warm ivory display plate inside the open frame. Silver hairlines belong outside its safe inset. A small leaf-shaped control can have a normal rounded hit target; focus should trace the actual button area, not every ornamental tendril. Reserve accent colour for a few state marks and keep labels literal.

### Light construction and risks

Hand-author a small vocabulary of tapered Bézier ribs, mirrored or repeated with limited variation. Use filled tapered shapes where line weight matters, narrow gradients for metal, and at most one small halo around the socket stone. Animate transforms, path reveal and a few matched control points. Do not build a full branching growth engine for the first study.

Risks: fine lines vanish at small sizes; arbitrary path interpolation tangles the ribs; apparent floating attachments can look disconnected. Keep load-bearing strokes visible at the smallest scale, use separate authored paths for major forms, and retain a solid frame detail showing the socket even with all glow disabled.

**First candidate:** iris clasp + fan-of-ribs shade. **Stretch candidate:** inlaid scarf fusion + enamel-ribbon shade, elegant enough to work without leaves or glow.

## 4. Wire, beads and stones

### What makes it itself

The references suggest multiple craft languages, not one copper theme:

- `refs/3.png`: dense coiled and woven collars around a malachite pendant; branching gemstone trees; a disciplined circular tree-of-life frame. The useful contrast is heavy bound edges against fine branching interiors.
- `refs/4.png`: a broad hoop, twisted trunk, radiating root attachments and scattered multicoloured beads. Its asymmetry and irregular bead spacing feel made by hand.
- `refs/5.png`: wire-and-bead insects, with antennae, bent feet and little coiled bases. A clasp can be a tiny sculptural object without turning the entire widget into a character.
- `refs/6.png`: copper coils cage beads with visible gaps. The bead remains recognisable through its enclosure; the cage suggests a natural capture mechanism.
- `refs/7.png`: unequal stones, pearls, spacer rings and deliberate changes of rhythm along strands. A beautiful strip need not use repeated identical spheres.
- `refs/8.png`: a wild dark wire nest with a blue cabochon, roses and hanging leaves. This is a compelling alternative to neat filigree: density around one focal stone, with irregular outlines.

Picture three families: a malachite instrument wrapped tightly at its ends; a light hoop frame whose roots form the lower casing; and an oxidised nest supporting one blue stone. Use warm copper, dark patina in overlaps, narrow worn highlights, cloudy stones and quieter pearls. Motion is bending and taking up slack; metal should not stretch like rubber.

**Cheap version:** a copper outline with identical coloured dots. **Overboard version:** the entire display woven shut, every bead sparkling, moving wires everywhere and ornament mistaken for buttons. Preserve a few distinctive construction details and a generous calm display well.

### Join alternatives

1. **Hook and eye — point attachment.** Approach: an open spiral hook lines up with a hammered eye. Commit: the hook turns a little in-plane, slips through and closes toward its own stem. Joined seam: a small readable S-shaped bridge with daylight around it. The hook itself is the release control. This should be the plain, practical craft option.
2. **Caged bead capture — enclosure attachment.** Approach: a stone bead at one end faces an open wire basket. Commit: four pre-bent ribs close around the stone, then a tiny collar slides into place. Joined seam: one shared bead visible through the cage. Releasing the collar opens the ribs. The bead belongs to one widget; the basket belongs to the other, making ownership legible when separated.
3. **Coil ferrule — binding attachment.** Approach: two straight wire bundles align end-to-end. Commit: a short helical sleeve advances over both bundles like a wound spring sliding into position. Joined seam: a dense copper barrel with dark gaps between turns. Release slides the sleeve home. Do not rotate the whole widgets or require a screw gesture.
4. **Branch graft — structural fusion.** Approach: two fine branching fans flatten toward matching paths. Commit: branches interleave, the small inner hoops retract, and selected roots become continuous across the join. Joined seam: a shared trunk with a few bead buds, while both displays keep their own quiet interiors. This is the tree reference translated into casing structure, not a tree picture behind the text.
5. **Bead-strand toggle — threaded attachment.** Approach: a short strand ending in a horizontal bar faces a wire loop. Commit: the bar turns edge-on, passes through and turns across the loop; beads settle into a short shallow curve. Joined seam: three unequal beads and a visible toggle. A small amount of drawn slack makes it distinct from the rigid hook. The mechanical joint remains fixed after settling.
6. **Nest interlock — entangling fusion.** Approach: two restrained clusters of loose ends lean together. Commit: three prominent wires weave over and under, and their darker background tangles resolve into one perimeter nest. Joined seam: an irregular but continuous dark knot with a single blue stone identifying the release point. Use a designed weave sequence, not random spaghetti generation at runtime.
7. **Beaded insect clasp — articulated attachment.** Approach: two pairs of wire feet open around matching frame tabs. Commit: a tiny abstract six-legged bead construction plants its feet on both tabs; its abdomen lowers to become the latch. Joined seam: one small sculptural bridge, not a moving pet. A wing-shaped lever lifts the feet for release. Limit this to a joyful variant; do not put a creature on every control.

### Shade alternatives

1. **Stone strand.** The surrounding wire frame withdraws into its end collars, leaving a horizontal strand of irregular stones supporting a slim opaque summary plaque. Restore draws the frame outward and down from those collars. Beads punctuate the strip; text stays on the plaque rather than being spread across individual beads.
2. **Flattened hoop.** The hoop's upper and lower arcs approach until they make a long narrow ellipse; roots gather along its bottom edge. Final strip: a wire oval holding a dark straight readout. Restore opens the oval and separates the roots. Maintain wire thickness rather than scaling the whole SVG vertically.
3. **Spool winding.** The outer wire outline draws back toward one small end spool while the display contracts to a rigid label rail. Final strip: rail, wound spool and one capped free end. Restore pays the wire out along a known outline. The visible winding is a short casing effect, not a simulated cable or the disappearance of functional controls into a bead.
4. **Branch fan.** Beaded branches rotate downward about their trunk until they lie nearly parallel beneath the summary spine. Final strip: a comb-like lower fringe with a few stones at its tips. Restore raises the branches into their authored positions. This preserves the tree's construction without needing a circle.
5. **Cabochon slide.** The nest and its large stone travel into an outer end pocket while two dark metal edges close around the summary. Final strip: a quiet patinated bar with a partially exposed blue cabochon at one end. Restore slides the nest back out around the expanded screen. This is a focal-stone design, distinct from the all-over strand.

### Morphs, angles and readable displays

A pendant-like compact form can become a broad hoop for reading, or a tall asymmetric frame with the cabochon at its shoulder. Keep stones the same size across modes and re-route the wires around them. Add or remove a small number of authored branch units at size thresholds. Never stretch an oval bead into a long pill because the widget grew wider.

Hook/eye and caged-bead joints can rotate around the eye or bead, initially testing ±60°. Toggle and strand joins can adjust through a narrower range, retaining a bounded length and recomputing the short curve. The ferrule stays straight; a hinged end fitting is a separate variant. The nest requires a visible pivot stone for angles, otherwise its interlocked wires should be treated as rigid.

Use dark patinated metal or a smooth matte stone-like plate behind text, even in an otherwise open hoop. A translucent gemstone cannot guarantee readable text against arbitrary desktop wallpaper. Keep texture, cloudy inclusions and bright stone highlights outside the display safe area. A pearl can indicate status, but pair it with a label or symbol. Checks is a small wrapped tablet in a wire-rimmed drawer cradle; its release grip remains distinct from decorative wire ends.

### Light construction and risks

Represent a wire with a dark under-stroke, a narrower copper stroke and a sparse offset highlight. Reuse coil and spacer symbols. Generate a bounded set of branch paths and bead positions once from a fixed seed; preserve that seed through every state. Stones need two or three gradients and a few clipped inclusion marks, not image assets. Solve apparent over-under crossings with short segment ordering or small local masks.

Risks: hundreds of strokes can dominate draw cost; thin highlights alias at non-integer positions; masks at every crossing can be more expensive than the wires; random branching can obscure controls or change identity between frames. Cap the visible branches, author the important crossings and remove secondary strands in shade. Keep the wild nest concentrated at an end cap. No per-bead blur, no perpetual bead shimmer and no spring simulation while idle.

**First candidate:** caged bead capture + stone-strand shade. **Stretch candidate:** branch-graft fusion + flattened-hoop shade. **Deliberate outlier:** the insect clasp, for testing how playful this family should become.

## 5. Hollow — an original subterranean storybook

### What makes it itself

Evoke the requested games' melancholy, delicacy and theatrical depth through original materials and drawing decisions: inked contours, pale shell plates, charcoal recesses, cool cavern colours, silk lines, worn carved fittings and a single restrained warm accent. No borrowed characters, masks, emblems, scenery or recognisable interface shapes.

Picture a flat, hand-drawn shell instrument with a chalk-white upper lip and a deep blue display well. A little rust-red woven pull breaks the cool palette. Alternative directions: a weathered gate made of dark reeds and pale pins; or an empty seed hull stitched to a silk-covered frame. Ornament suggests a world outside the instrument without drawing a whole cave on the desktop.

Motion can be economical and slightly theatrical: a taut thread releases, a shell edge slides home, a brief contour smear resolves. Light is largely painted into the forms. A tiny one-shot dust accent is conceivable, but not required for the identity.

**Cheap version:** a black panel with a white skull and particles. **Overboard version:** unbounded fog, trembling outlines, flourishes longer than the interaction, and shapes too close to existing game art. The drawing should still feel deliberate with all effects turned off.

### Join alternatives

1. **Shell lap — fusion.** Approach: pale overlapping lips reveal complementary dark grooves. Commit: one lip slides beneath the other and the two ink silhouettes settle into a continuous outer contour. Joined seam: a thin dark stepped line with one small chalk release peg. A short, decisive motion makes this feel like hard shell rather than soft goo.
2. **Silk tension seam — binding attachment.** Approach: reinforced pinholes line up along both ends. Commit: two fine threads cross the gap and tighten into an X, pulling the edges close without erasing them. Joined seam: a pale stitched X over a dark slit, with a rust-red pull loop. Release loosens the loop and draws the thread back. More austere than felt's thick blanket stitches.
3. **Reed gate bolt — sliding attachment.** Approach: a small carved bar points toward an empty keeper on the other casing. Commit: the bar slides horizontally through the keeper and a peg drops behind it. Joined seam: a miniature dark lintel secured by one pale pin. The pin is the release control. This gives the family a practical, weathered mechanism rather than relying entirely on supernatural effects.
4. **Shadow dovetail — silhouette fusion.** Approach: two jagged end silhouettes cast matching narrow shadows. Commit: those shadows meet first, then the solid edges advance into them; the joining line becomes one ink-black notch. Joined seam: a precise negative-space key shape outlined on focus. This uses drawn staging, not smoky fluid merging. Retain the notch so users can find the joint on a dark desktop.
5. **Seed-pod collar — enclosing attachment.** Approach: a split husk opens around a polished endpoint. Commit: its two pale plates close with a slight offset, and a short dark band settles around their waist. Joined seam: an asymmetric seed capsule across the gap. Release lifts the waist band. Shape it from an original elongated botanical form rather than a familiar mask or face.
6. **Silk suspension bridge — remote attachment.** Approach: two small upper pins face each other across an intentional gap. Commit: a taut top line forms, then a shallow lower arc supports a tiny central latch. Joined seam: an open bridge with visible anchors, keeping the widgets separate in silhouette. Release at the central latch retracts the bridge. No swinging widgets; the illustrated suspension suggests delicacy while the placement remains stable.

### Shade alternatives

1. **Nested husks.** Three overlapping shell plates slide upward under the nameplate. Final strip: a pale upper rim, dark summary slot and two thin lower contour lines. Restore extends the plates in reverse order. Most convincing everyday option for the shell material.
2. **Silk blind.** A dark woven membrane rolls upward onto a narrow pale rod as the lower casing withdraws. Final strip: a dark band edged by that rod, with a small red pull at one end. Restore draws the membrane down and returns the casing. Do not roll the live text; switch between summary and full display.
3. **Ink closure.** A horizontal painted shadow closes from top and bottom, masking the full casing as its silhouette retracts to a thin line. Final strip: a brush-edged black plaque with crisp white text. Restore opens the painted aperture. A theatrical option that can use masks and opacity without particles.
4. **Reed bundle.** Long frame elements rotate in-plane and stack beneath one horizontal rail. Final strip: a bound bundle with a smooth inset label. Restore unfolds the reeds around the display like a spare stage set. The bundles should occupy the casing perimeter, not cover text during the entire animation.
5. **Hanging banner tuck.** The lower shell and display recede behind a retained woven name banner; its two small end weights rise to the banner's baseline. Final strip: dark cloth between pale end weights. Restore lowers the weights and brings the shell out beneath it. This warmer, textile version offers an alternative to hard shell everywhere.

### Morphs, angles and readable displays

A low shell can extend additional plates to become tall; a narrow reed instrument can open into a wide frame. Reuse a small set of authored contours so the drawing stays intentional. Scale the empty areas between ribs, not the width of the ink outline. Checks is a tiny shell tablet seated in a velvet-dark drawer recess, with its name and eject control visible when the drawer opens.

An overlapping shell joint is straight unless explicitly built around a circular peg. The seed collar can expose such a peg, testing ±40°. Silk-bridge anchors can support a wider authored range, but their spans must avoid the content rectangles and maintain the same fixed socket centres. At a changed angle, recompute only the connector drawing; do not add a free swinging simulation.

Use near-white text on an opaque blue-black well, or dark text on a flat chalk panel. Keep rough strokes on the outside boundary, never on glyph edges. No fog or screen grain across the words. Status can use small warm stitches or seed-shaped markers, always reinforced by text. Background atmosphere belongs in the preview stage, not in every floating widget's bounds.

### Light construction and risks

Use a few filled SVG silhouettes, slightly irregular authored outlines, flat shadow shapes and restrained gradients. A reusable static stipple pattern can suggest wear. Reveal strokes or translate plates for motion; use a tightly bounded mask for ink closure. A two- or three-pose transition can give a drawn cadence without animating an outline forever.

Risks: low-value shapes can disappear against real wallpaper; open bridges can look like workflow links; noisy linework can double the path count; glow and fog can consume large offscreen surfaces. Ensure the solid casing and socket markers work without atmosphere, label attachment state in the study, and keep any dust accent optional and finite. Original silhouettes are an explicit design task, not a late recolour of borrowed art.

**First candidate:** shell-lap fusion + nested-husk shade. **Stretch candidate:** shadow-dovetail fusion + ink closure, for the strongest graphic departure from physical hardware.

## Combining skins

There are at least five useful models. They should be compared as different answers to who owns the seam, the silhouette and the material. Simply allowing every gradient to mix with every other gradient would lose the craft logic.

### Model A — each widget keeps its skin; a bridge translates between sockets

The connection owns a small adapter with an endpoint supplied by each skin and a bounded centre. Docking reveals the two endpoint fittings, closes the central latch, and leaves both casings intact. The adapter owns the release target and angle limit, constrained by both sockets. It has no display or independent widget status.

Examples:

- **Gothic + felt:** an iron ring set into stone, a sewn felt loop, and one small pin through both. Pale masonry against moss wool could be exceptionally tactile.
- **Elven + wire:** a silver iris closes around a copper-caged moonstone. Keep silver on one side, copper on the other; let the stone mediate rather than dissolving the metals together.
- **Hollow + gothic:** a pale shell peg enters a weathered bronze keeper on a stone pier. The silhouettes remain respectively drawn and architectural.

On shade, each widget uses its own strip, while the bridge retains a small socket carrier. At angles, the adapter is a visibly articulated component, not an arbitrary stretch between unrelated ends.

**What breaks:** bespoke adapters for every pair would grow combinatorially; incompatible drawing scales can make a tiny elven clasp look absurd beside a massive stone corbel; the bridge may grow into the bulky empty separator the prototype rejected. Start with a few endpoint classes—eye, peg, collar, flat face—and a very small central vocabulary. An unsupported pair should show unavailable compatibility or use an explicit universal pin adapter, not silently invent fusion.

### Model B — compose material, construction, ornament and palette

One widget receives an authored construction family, a material treatment, an ornament family and a palette. Construction owns thickness, joints and motion; material changes rendering; ornament follows declared attachment zones; palette maps semantic colours. These are constrained combinations, not four unlimited dropdowns.

Examples:

- **Felt material + gothic construction cues:** a wool chapel-shaped case with appliqué window recesses and a button-loop joint. It behaves like a sewn object; its “stone courses” are layered felt pieces.
- **Elven construction + wire material:** tapered copper bows, a few pearl spacers and an iris made from bent wire ribs. Elegant structural lines survive without becoming a random jewellery pile.
- **Hollow drawing + gothic ornament:** a chalk-and-ink arcade with sparse weathered friezes, using nested shell plates for shade. The carving is simplified into the same drawn vocabulary.
- **Wire construction + felt ornament:** a metal hoop with a padded label sewn across its interior. Wool softens the display support but does not replace the hoop's structural joint.

**What breaks:** material metaphors contradict motion, ornament crosses a display safe inset, or changing palette erases functional contrast. A stone-textured pouch that squashes like felt should be an intentional surreal variant, not an accidental default. Each combination needs an allowed construction recipe and semantic colour checks. The generic parts contract still identifies casing, display, grip, buttons and sockets; author-specific forms can opt into richer recipes.

### Model C — a shared outer chassis with independent inset skins

An explicit group frame carries the docking skeleton. Widgets slide into its bays like tablets. The frame has one structural aesthetic; each inset keeps its display treatment and a restrained signature edge. This generalises containment rather than pretending every pair can fuse.

Examples:

- A **gothic arcade** carries an elven enamel tablet and a wire-wrapped stone tablet, each separated by a slim stone pier.
- A **felt tool roll** carries a hollow shell instrument and a wire bead instrument, with wool pockets visibly supporting them.
- An **elven crescent chassis** holds pale felt inserts and a small dark hollow insert, using fine metal ribs to organise the whole group.

A group shade can roll, telescope or fold the chassis into one rail with a separately labelled segment per widget. Individual removal restores each full casing. Jarvis's existing Checks drawer remains its own nested containment relationship; the outer frame must not take ownership of Checks accidentally.

**What breaks:** independent widget identities disappear, nested bays confuse release and eject actions, or the group frame becomes the largest object on the desktop. Limit framing to one outer layer in the first experiment. Treat this as an explicit optional group object with an explicit dissolve action, not something proximity creates automatically. Angled layouts need articulated chassis segments; a rigid arcade remains straight.

### Model D — a deliberate material transition at the seam

Each widget stays in its original skin except for a short jointly authored transition zone. The join tells a tiny transformation story instead of using a discrete adapter. This is the most expressive and least universal model.

Examples:

- **Felt → wire:** embroidered thread gathers into darker strands, becomes copper wire, then winds around the neighbouring bead. The transition happens in an outer hem, clear of text.
- **Gothic → elven:** carved stone tracery thins into polished ribs, with the last stone cusp becoming a silver leaf tip.
- **Wire → hollow:** a dense dark wire cluster simplifies into ink contours and settles into a shell lip, retaining one pale stone as a common anchor.

Commitment reveals the transition from both ends toward the middle. Separation reverses it and restores both original caps. A shared seed and a stored recipe keep the result consistent; no runtime image generation or unconstrained geometry synthesis is needed.

**What breaks:** it can look as if a widget is infected or changing skin without consent; material may seem to melt instead of being made; arbitrary pairs are not meaningfully reversible. Offer only a few named, explicitly selected recipes. During shade, reduce the zone to the same small anchor motif. At angles, require a pivot in that recipe rather than warping the transition.

### Model E — one common mounting rail, individually dressed objects

Widgets connect to a shared rail or cord, not directly to one another. Unlike the outer chassis, this contributes only a spine and small clamps; it does not enclose displays. The rail is an explicit layout object and is visually different from a data connection.

Examples:

- A dark **gothic iron rail** carries felt button loops, wire hooks and an elven sliding saddle.
- A restrained **wire strand** carries a hollow shell peg and a felt tab, with one silver elven clasp setting the group's angle.
- A pale **elven spine** holds gothic stone collars and wire-caged beads, leaving their bodies spatially separate.

Shaded widgets become labelled segments along the rail. Expanded widgets project from it while retaining their local clamp. A segmented rail can turn at explicit hinges; it should not snake freely while dragging.

**What breaks:** the rail reads as a workflow graph, produces dangling clutter, or makes moving one widget unexpectedly reorder all the others. Keep it short, declare its ordering behaviour, give each clamp an obvious release, and never draw signal pulses. It is a different assembly mode worth exploring after direct docking, not a replacement for the baseline.

### What I would compare first

Build the same mixed pair as **A, adapter**, and **D, transition**: gothic Jarvis beside wire GitDiscuss. In A, a black iron eye receives a copper hook; in D, the final carved stone branch becomes a copper-wrapped root. The displays and socket positions stay identical. This isolates whether mixed skins should feel like collecting objects or composing one crafted object.

Then try felt Jarvis with hollow GitDiscuss in **C, shared tool roll**. That pairing tests whether a common support can reconcile radically different rendering styles. Keep wire Checks in Jarvis's own drawer so the two containment levels are visible rather than hypothetical.

## Keeping the eventual studies light

These are implementation hypotheses and design limits, not measured performance claims. The HTML studies would be self-contained comparisons, not evidence that a browser-per-widget architecture meets the native footprint goal.

- **Share the interaction model.** Style the generic named parts and expose extra authored forms separately. Preserve page state and input semantics while swapping casing graphics. Keep joining, animation progress, shading and drawer ownership distinct so any required state can be frozen reliably.
- **Author a little, repeat carefully.** Reuse gradients, patterns and SVG symbols within each self-contained page. Prefer a few strong silhouettes over hundreds of decorative nodes. Procedural generation runs once or on a size threshold, with deterministic seeds; it does not run every frame.
- **Spend motion only on change.** Update geometry during drag and finite transitions; stop animation frames after settling. No idle shimmer, fibre movement, breathing, dust field or perpetually evaluated physics. If one-shot effects add nothing to comprehension, remove them.
- **Keep textures tiny and shared.** An optional shared 64 × 64 RGBA tile is about 16 KiB before other costs. A single 390 × 130 RGBA casing cache at 2× resolution is about 0.77 MiB before compositor and object overhead, already close to the README's per-widget aspiration. Whole-widget raster caches are therefore not an automatic optimisation. Test shared tiles and vector drawing first.
- **Bound expensive effects.** Filter only a small casing or connector region; avoid full-stage blur and filters wrapping text. A filter-free version must retain the material identity through silhouette, value and construction. SVG reuse reduces source duplication but does not guarantee cheap rendering; measure drawing and memory separately.
- **Simplify by physical size.** Drop secondary fibres, roots, carving and stipple in shade. Keep beads, buttons and socket grips recognisable. Do not reduce text or target sizes merely to preserve ornament.
- **Verify the hard cases later.** Compare full and shade on light and dark wallpaper, high display scaling, reduced motion, keyboard navigation, an angled pair with its drawer open, one-sided shade, and many idle widgets. Inspect frame time during dragging and memory after repeated mode changes. Native click-through and cross-platform filter support remain separate engineering questions.

## Top picks — what I most want to build

1. **Wire: caged-bead docking + stone-strand shade.** An oxidised copper basket closes around a cloudy green stone; the joined pair shares one tangible jewel. When shaded, both elaborate casings resolve to unequal stones supporting narrow dark status plaques. This uses three reference ideas together—cages, wrapped stones and strands—without copying a photographed object. It is the strongest candidate for a distinctive family at both large and tiny sizes.
2. **Felt: button-loop docking + pocket-tuck shade.** Oatmeal Jarvis and indigo GitDiscuss meet through one apricot loop and a pale button; Checks sits in a moss pocket below. Shade leaves tidy double-layer fabric tabs. This is warm, plausible and immediately readable. It tests whether convincing material can come from thickness and pressure rather than heavy texture. Follow it with needle-felt fusion only if the quieter version feels too ordinary.
3. **Gothic: keystone fusion + clerestory shade.** Two sunlit limestone cases lose their inner capitals and become one low arcade, with a brass wedge seating above the seam. Shade retains small ruby and amber glass lights over crisp dark status slots. I want this specifically in pale stone first: it gives the set a different value structure and avoids making every intricate style another dark panel.
4. **Elven: iris clasp + fan-of-ribs shade.** Three long silver petals close around a pale endpoint; on shade, the case's ribs gather into a delicate stack at one end of a dark reading rail. This gives the elven option visible engineering and beautiful negative space. The risk is line visibility, so its first proof should be at actual desktop size rather than enlarged artwork.
5. **Hollow: shell-lap fusion + nested-husk shade, with ink closure as the challenger.** Chalk plates overlap with a single decisive slide; the strip becomes a dark slit under a pale lip and one rust-red pull. It should look authored even with no glow or texture. Ink closure is worth comparing because it challenges the assumption that every material transformation must be mechanically literal.
6. **Mixed: gothic stone turning into wire roots.** Compare a simple iron-to-copper adapter with the carved-branch-to-wire transition using identical content and positions. The adapter tests useful coexistence; the transition tests a new shared object. That comparison could settle more about cross-skin design than a large gallery of unrelated colour combinations.

Keep the beaded insect clasp, floating moonstone joint and wild wire nest as deliberate outliers in the idea set. They are valuable because they test playfulness, magic and disorder separately. I would build them after the first candidates establish readable displays and dependable docking, then judge whether their additional personality earns the space it occupies.
